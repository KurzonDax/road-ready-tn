# Building a lesson

How a Section's lesson (`data/lessons/section-N.json`) gets written, checked and handed over. The format and the drawing kit are in `docs/specs/lessons.md`; this document is the judgement that the format cannot hold. Every rule here was set by the owner while reviewing the first lesson, Section 3, one question at a time. The Learner is his son: 18, autistic, fully intelligent, reads every word literally, preparing for the Tennessee knowledge test. Write for an intelligent adult who takes each sentence at its exact meaning.

## Steps

1. **Read** `docs/specs/lessons.md`, this document, the Section's ten facts in `data/sections.json` and `data/questions.json` (their `explanation` and `manual_quote` are the truth each question must match), and the manual passage they come from in `data/manual/sections-b-c.txt`. Then read `data/lessons/section-3.json` as the worked example.
2. **Write** the lesson as an Opus subagent with this document and the spec in its brief. Ten to fifteen questions: two or three cases that share the Section's principle, the principle named, the remaining facts, a closing principle. The principle in the middle is asked only when the situations share something worth naming that was not said; when they do not, as with Section 2's flashing lights, there is no middle principle and the closing question carries it.
3. **Lint**: `python3 tests/validate_data.py` prints `OK`. The lint (`check_lesson_wording`) is the enforced form of the language rules below; a lesson that fails it does not build.
4. **Audit** with a second Opus subagent that reads every question as the Learner will, against the checklists under Questions and Language, and edits the file. Then lint again.
5. **Build and check**: `python3 build.py`, then `tests/test_app.py check_lessons` passes (run with a Python that has Playwright; `CHROMIUM_PATH` picks an installed Chromium).
6. **Sheet**: `python3 tools/lesson_sheet.py N reports/lessons/section-N.html` writes the lesson as one page. Read the whole page yourself, every wrong path included, and fix what you find. Regenerate.
7. **Hand over the sheet** to the owner. A lesson is reviewed as that page, never by tapping through the app. Done when he has the page and nothing on it breaks a rule below.

## Questions

A lesson is a sequence of situations the Learner decides, not text he reads. Each question is one decision.

- **One question form, so the marks mean one thing.** Every tap question asks "Who goes first? Tap the car." (or "Tap the person or your car."). The green check marks the one that goes. When a scene has two that go first, redesign the scene until exactly one does; put the wider rule in the explanation. Section 3's question 2 is the worked example: one car on the main road, and "both directions" in the explanation. In a lesson about signs (Section 1) the one form is "Which sign means X? Tap the sign.", and the green check marks the sign that means it. Exactly one sign in the scene means it. In a lesson about passing (Section 7) the one form is "You are the red car. You want to pass the blue car. Which lane do you use to pass? Tap the lane.", with a lane box in each lane he could use, and the green check marks the lane he passes in. In the parking lesson (Section 8) the forms are "You want to park as close to the fire hydrant as the law allows. Which space do you use? Tap the space.", with three parking spaces along the curb whose distances the prompt states and the drawing measures, and the green check marks the space at exactly the manual's distance (15 feet from the hydrant), the other two standing at the test's own wrong numbers, and "Which way do you turn your front wheels? Tap the wheels.", with two wheel pictures side by side, and the green check marks the turn the manual gives. In the interstate lesson (Section 9) the lane form is "You are the red car and driving slower than the other cars. Tap the lane you must use.": the prompt begins "You are the red car", states the one fact the decision turns on, and ends "Tap the lane you must use.", with a lane box in every lane he could use (three for slower traffic, the right lane and the deceleration lane for an exit, the two lanes past a stopped emergency vehicle or a closing lane), and the green check marks the lane the manual gives. The ramp question keeps "Who goes first? Tap the car.", and the blue car already on the interstate goes first. The alcohol lesson (Section 11) has no tap question, because none of its facts has exactly one correct thing in a drawing: every question is a choice about a stated situation ("You drank three beers in the past hour.", "You are 18."), and the counterintuitive facts (a 12-ounce beer holds about as much alcohol as a 1-ounce shot, only time sobers) are asked before they are told. In the night and weather lesson (Section 10) most facts are decisions with nothing to draw, so most questions are choices about a stated situation, and the two tap forms are "You are the red car, driving at night with your high beams on. The blue car coming toward you is 500 feet away, and the green car behind it is 1,000 feet away. Which car is close enough that you must dim your high beams for it? Tap the car.", where the green check marks the car within the manual's 500 feet, and "You are the red car, and your car does not have anti-lock brakes. The rear of your car is sliding to the left. Which way do you turn the steering wheel? Tap the steering wheel.", with two steering wheel pictures side by side, where the green check marks the wheel turned the way the rear is sliding. It says "the steering wheel", never "the wheel", which a literal reader can take for a road wheel.
- **The drawing explains the question, and the prompt says the rest.** The owner rejected the first Section 7 prompt, which described in words what the drawing already showed (two lanes each way, the lane nearest the right edge, a slow car ahead, the broken white line, the two solid yellow lines) and asked him to use the drawing instead: paint the cars, say "You are the red car. You want to pass the blue car.", and ask. A sentence that describes what he can see is struck.
- **The prompt states every fact the decision needs.** What controls the intersection, in words, every time: the sign, the light and its color, or "There is no stop sign and no yield sign." Who arrived first. Which way each vehicle is going, and which way the Learner wants to go. How close the other vehicle is when it matters. The drawing shows the same facts; it never carries one alone.
- **Targets are named from the driver's seat.** "your car", "the car on your left", "the car coming toward you", "the person". Never a position on the page or a letter. In a passing lesson (Section 7) the cars are painted and named by color, because the owner asked for the drawing to explain the question: "You are the red car. You want to pass the blue car. Which lane do you use to pass? Tap the lane." His car is red, the other car blue, and the prompt says only what the drawing cannot show. A sign is the one exception: it is named by its shape and color ("the red eight-sided sign", "the yellow diamond"), because that is what the question is about, and the prompt lists every sign the same way. The colors are the sign's real colors: a yield sign is "a red and white three-sided sign pointing down", never "a red three-sided sign".
- **Every wrong pick gets its own explanation.** For each wrong target or option, `then.wrong` holds what the Learner reads under "Incorrect. You picked X.": why that pick is wrong, then what is correct, in at most three sentences. One explanation shared by every pick (Section 3 to 5) read on the sheet as no explanation at all for a wrong answer, and the owner said so. A question with two wrong targets has two paths; write and read both.
- **A method is used before it is asked about.** A decision he can reason out from the facts stated (who entered whose road) may be asked cold. A method he has never seen (counting seconds past a sign post) may not: the first question states the count as something that happened and asks only what follows from it ("and then your car passed the same sign post. How far behind are you?"), the next questions use the count, and "what do you do when the car ahead passes a pole?" comes last, after he has used it. Section 4's drafts asked the method first, and the owner saw that he was being questioned on something he had never been shown.
- **A choose question asks about the scene drawn.** "May you drive into the intersection now?" with the cars stopped past it in the drawing, never "if you cannot get all the way through".
- **A principle question names its cases.** "When you drove out of the driveway onto the main road, and when your road ended at the main road with no sign, who waited each time?" The closing question is short and exact: "Every question in this lesson came down to one thing. What was it?" Its answer says what the situations share that the prompt did not already say, and the explanation names it once, briefly, then says why. An answer that repeats the prompt, and an explanation that repeats the answer, tell him the same thing three times and teach nothing. The situations it names must all point the same way: a principle that holds for two of them and needs an exception for the third contradicts itself to a literal reader. Section 2's drafts asked what the color yellow told him after a steady yellow meant stop and a flashing yellow meant go on, then what the two flashing lights had in common, and both were pointless, so the question was removed.
- **The options of a choose or principle question** are complete sentences or a plain Yes or No, and exactly one is defensible on the facts stated.

## Language

Precise means exact, never exhaustive. A sentence that lists eight situations is as wrong as one that names none.

- **Correct and incorrect.** "Correct." and "Incorrect. You picked your car." In a driving app "right" is a direction and nothing else.
- **What the driver does, in his words.** "You wait and let it pass." "You stay stopped until it has gone across." "You stop before the crosswalk and wait until the person has crossed." The word "yield" belongs to a yield sign only. The questions in `data/questions.json` keep the test's own wording, because that is what the test says.
- **They are questions.** "This lesson has 12 questions." The button is "Go on". The count is "1 of 12".
- **Name what you refer to.** A situation is named by what happened in it, never pointed at as "both cases", "these", or "the one above".
- **Short sentences, few of them.** A sentence is at most 30 words; a prompt at most 60. The question itself asks one thing ("Which sign means you slow down and let the traffic on the other road go first?"), and the conditions go in the explanation. An explanation is at most three sentences: what happens, then why. It names the correct one once; the lead never repeats it.
- **A light is named by its color and whether it flashes.** "a steady yellow light", "a flashing red light", "a steady green left arrow". Never "round": there are no square lights, so the word says nothing. Never "your light": the Learner does not own a light, he comes up to one. "You are coming up to a steady yellow light." "The light ahead of you has just turned green." "The arrow over your lane" names the lane he is in, and is fine.
- **Tennessee roads, plainly.** "the main road", "a stop sign with an ALL WAY sign under it", "a traffic light that is not working". No idioms, no hedges, no exclamation marks, no semicolons, no praise.
- **The manual stays in the data.** Its sentences and page numbers verify every fact and never appear on a Learner screen.

The lint refuses: yield (except "yield sign"), right for correct, wrong as a verdict (the test's own "the wrong way", a direction, is allowed), round for a light, your light, step, screen, case, unnamed references, semicolons, exclamation marks, verdict and praise words, sentences and prompts over the caps, and a tap question that draws an intersection without saying what controls it.

## Drawings

Top-down, schematic, from the kit in `docs/specs/lessons.md`, drawn as Tennessee roads are.

- **The traffic signal is a vertical head, red on top, on a span across the road**, over the far side of the intersection, centered on the lanes it controls. On the standard intersection with the Learner heading north that is `x` 160, `y` 121. A signal on a corner post belongs downtown in a city, not here.
- **All-way stops have a stop sign with an ALL WAY plate at every corner**, and the prompt says so.
- **Every car sits in the correct lane for its heading** (right-hand traffic: northbound in the east lane, southbound in the west lane, eastbound in the south lane, westbound in the north lane), turned toward its heading.
- **The Learner's car is marked `you`** and a `path` arrow shows where he wants to go whenever the prompt says it.
- **A sign in a sign lesson is drawn by shape and color alone** (`roadsign`), with no words or symbols on it, beside the road the Learner is on. A drawing never gives away what the question asks: a question that asks what shape or color a sign is draws no sign, and asks instead what a drawn sign means.
- **A question about which lane is a close-up.** The owner rejected the Section 6 drafts that showed the whole intersection from far away with two small car outlines pushed apart along the street so that they could be tapped. The drawing is zoomed in on the corner instead: lanes 60 to 70 wide, the Learner heading north, and a lane box (`lane`) filling each lane he could use, side by side, with nothing written on it: text inside the boxes was too small to read, and the prompt names the lanes. The boxes are the targets, and they are never spread apart to make room for a thumb.
- **A passing question draws the whole situation, and nothing that answers it.** The bicycle, the motorcycle and the truck are kit parts (`bike`, `truck`) the prompt names. A distance the question asks for (3 feet beside a bicycle, 200 feet behind a truck) and a truck's shaded blind spots (`zone`) are drawn only after he has answered, in `then.parts`; a distance the prompt states as a fact of the situation (100 feet behind the truck) is drawn as a `measure` in the scene. A hill or a curve he cannot see past is a shaded stretch with a label, and the prompt says he cannot see it.
- **A hand signal is drawn from behind.** The owner asked for a drawing of the bicyclist's signal and for all three signals to be covered (Section 6). A bent arm cannot be seen from above, so the `bicyclist` part is drawn from behind: the rider as the driver behind sees him, the arm in the position the prompt names, one question per signal.
- **A hill is drawn from the side, and the wheels from above.** A slope cannot be seen from above either, so the `hill` part (Section 8) is the other view that is not top-down: the Learner's car on the slope, facing the way the prompt says, with the curb drawn and labelled when the prompt says there is one. The wheel pictures under it (`wheels`) are two close-up box targets side by side, the car from above with its front wheels turned toward the curb in one and away in the other, nothing written in either, so the Learner picks the turn by seeing it.
- **A distance question draws the distances it asks about, and the correct one is the manual's number.** The parking spaces (`space`) are box targets along the curb, each with a `measure` in the scene saying how far it is from the hydrant, the crosswalk, the sign or the crossing, and the prompt states the same distances, so the drawing never carries them alone. The space he should tap stands at exactly the manual's number (15 feet from the hydrant), and the other two at the test's own wrong numbers (5 and 30). The owner rejected a first draft whose correct space stood at 20 feet, a number that merely satisfies the rule: it taught an arbitrary number in place of the manual's. A number is always taught as the manual's number.
- **The interstate is one roadway at a time.** A question on the interstate (Section 9) draws one roadway of the divided highway as a close-up (`road` with `oneway` 2 or 3), the Learner heading north, with the median strip at its left edge and the shoulder (`shoulder`) at its right when the prompt names them. An entrance or an exit is a `ramp` joining the east edge at an angle, with the acceleration or deceleration lane beside the roadway, and a lane box in that lane is the target. A work zone is an orange diamond `roadsign` beside the road and a row of cones (`cone`) angled across the closing lane, the first cone at the road's edge. A stopped emergency vehicle is a `car` with `emergency: true`, white, on the shoulder, and the prompt says it is stopped with its lights flashing. Only the crossover question draws both roadways, with the median in two pieces and the paved gap between them, because that question is about the gap. The missed exit is the off-ramp behind the Learner's car, labelled "Your exit".
- **Night is a tone, and the beam is what the driver can see.** A night question (Section 10) lists `night` right after the road, a dark sheet over the road and its markings, then a `headlight` beam from every car whose lights are on, then the cars. The beam is the stretch of road the driver can see, so the question about seeing a person at night draws the person past the end of your low beam, and the prompt says so. The dimming question draws your high beam and two cars coming toward you with low beams, a `measure` for each distance (500 feet to the near one, the test's 1,000 feet to the far one), and the prompt states both distances; after the tap `then.parts` redraws the scene with your beam dimmed. The following question is the same at 500 feet behind one car. Flood water is a `water` patch across the road with a "Flood water" label, and the turn back is a `path` arrow drawn after the choice. The stalled car stands on the `rails` with the `crossbuck` at the roadside, and after the choice a `ped` stands well away from the tracks.
- **A skid is a tilted car, and the steering wheel is the target.** The skidding car (Section 10) heads north with `tilt` so that its rear has swung to the page's left, which is the driver's left, and a `path` arrow at its rear shows which way the rear is sliding, while the prompt says "The rear of your car is sliding to the left." The two `steer` boxes under the road are close-ups side by side, the steering wheel as the driver holds it turned to the left in one and to the right in the other, nothing written in either, so the Learner picks the turn by seeing it, and after the tap the car is drawn straight.
- **A drink is drawn from the side, and the hours as a timeline.** Section 11 has almost nothing to draw on a road. The beer and the shot (`drink`) are the third view that is not top-down: the two containers at their real relative sizes with their sizes written under them, so the beer looks like far more drink, and after the answer the same small square of alcohol under each. The three beers in an hour stand over a `timeline` from Now to 4 hours with nothing shaded, and after the answer the band to 3 hours is shaded. The BAC numbers, the penalty, the skills and the law have no drawing: the prompt states the situation and the age, and the options are the test's own.
- **At most three vehicles and one person.** Roads get a label when the prompt names them ("Main road", "Driveway").
- **After acting, the player marks the drawing**: the correct one gets a green ring and a check, a wrong tap a red ring and a cross. A lesson never draws its own marks.

## What the Learner sees after acting

Fixed by the player; a lesson supplies only `then.say` and, when the scene changes, `then.parts`.

1. One line: "Correct." in green, or "Incorrect. You picked your car." in red. A wrong choice reads "Incorrect." alone, because the list under it marks the pick.
2. The drawing, marked.
3. The explanation, once: after a wrong pick, the one written for that pick (`then.wrong`), after the correct one `then.say`.

Nothing else: no repeated prompt, no rule paragraph, no quote, no hint.

## Rejected, so it is not proposed again

Each of these was built and taken out: the manual's sentence and page on a Learner screen; a rule paragraph under the explanation; "Next step" and "step" anywhere; "screens" for questions; "Not your car." as the whole result; "Right." for correct; "yield" for waiting; "In both cases"; a prompt listing every situation in the lesson; a prompt that describes in words what its drawing shows (Section 7's first draft); dashed rings as marks; a horizontal signal head; a signal on a corner post; a drawing with no sign and a prompt that does not say so; a rule shown and then its own question asked immediately; a lesson as text with a Next button; a Section framed as a story or a trip; physics the test does not ask (stopping distance, feet per second); asking the owner to proofread one question at a time; a principle question whose answer restates its situations, or names something they share that does not matter (Section 2's flashing lights); a zoomed-out lane question with small car outlines staggered apart so that they can be tapped, and lane names written inside the lane boxes (Section 6); one explanation shared by the correct and every wrong pick, which read as no explanation for a wrong answer. A correct answer at an arbitrary number that merely satisfies the rule (a parking space 20 feet from the hydrant, Section 8), in place of the manual's own number.

## Done when

- `python3 tests/validate_data.py` prints `OK` and `tests/test_app.py check_lessons` passes on the built page.
- Every one of the Section's ten facts is taught by a question whose answer agrees with the fact's `explanation`.
- Every tap question asks who goes first and has exactly one target that does.
- Every wrong path has been read in the sheet and its explanation answers the pick.
- The owner has the sheet for the Section.
